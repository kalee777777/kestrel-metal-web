Fence Liability: Escaped Animals

May 28, 2025 5 min read
Back to Blog & News

The role of fencing extends beyond merely delineating farm boundaries. Proper fencing also serves to reduce accidents involving livestock and vehicles—collisions that are all too common on public roads traversing agricultural land.

Most states have enacted laws specifying how fences should be constructed and defining the extent of farmer liability when livestock escape and cause personal injury or property damage.

Generally, farm owners bear responsibility for all injuries and damages caused by escaped livestock, unless they can demonstrate that their fencing was in good condition and they were not at fault. In most states, the law places the burden on farm owners to ensure livestock remain contained.

Legal Classification of Animals

Laws typically categorize animals into two groups: wild animals and domestic animals.

Farm livestock is generally considered harmless to humans and therefore classified as domestic animals. If you own livestock, the law imposes certain legal responsibilities on you for the animals' behavior, including:

  • Your livestock breaking through fencing onto a roadway, causing injury or accidents
  • Your livestock breaking through fencing onto neighboring farmland and destroying crops
  • Your livestock, such as an unruly bull, breaking through fencing and injuring someone on your property

In these situations, litigation is likely to follow. In most states, farm owners found liable may face substantial fines or imprisonment.

If your livestock escape and engage in any of the above behaviors, you should consult an attorney to understand your legal position and how to develop a defense strategy.

Fence-In vs. Fence-Out Rules

Most states in the U.S. follow "fence-in" or "fence-out" rules when determining a farm owner's fence liability.

States outside the American West generally follow fence-in rules. This means farm owners are legally required to contain their livestock with fencing. Most laws in these states also require adjacent landowners to equally install and properly maintain all partition fences.

Challenges with Partition Fences

This rule can create complications when a livestock operator shares a partition fence with another landowner who doesn't raise livestock and therefore doesn't need fencing.

If they fail to equally participate in fence maintenance and escaped livestock pass through the portion of fence they should have equally maintained, causing property damage or personal injury, they cannot sue the livestock owner for damages.

If escaped livestock escape through a fence defect and damage a neighbor's property, the non-livestock owner may also bear liability, as they share equal responsibility for fence maintenance with the livestock owner.

To ensure proper accountability, some states have implemented special provisions that further narrow the scope of liability, allowing only the livestock owner or the adjacent non-livestock owner to be held responsible.

How Liability Is Typically Determined

In most cases, liability is determined under common law negligence theories. The injured party or plaintiff must prove that the livestock owner acted irresponsibly, causing the animals to escape.

In most instances, the injured party submits evidence proving negligence based on various facts and circumstances surrounding the incident. These claims include, but are not limited to:

  • Livestock escaped due to damaged or poorly maintained facilities, fences, or enclosures
  • Inadequate or improper staff training to control or handle escaped animals
  • Improper or inadequate livestock husbandry causing them to escape
  • Defects existing even when fences, facilities, or enclosures appeared intact—such as loose latches, insufficient fence height, or inappropriate fencing materials
  • Owner's failure to take appropriate corrective measures despite multiple prior escapes
Some states have stricter laws. For example, Michigan has an "Animals Running at Large" statute that imposes civil liability on livestock owners for property damage regardless of any evidence of improper conduct.

The injured party is entitled to reasonable compensation for their losses.

Liability Varies by Escape Location and Method

Your liability scope depends on where and how your livestock escape. Typically, most cases involve damage to neighbors' crops or livestock dangerously walking on or crossing highways.

Neighbor's Crops Damaged

In the past, some states, such as Missouri, operated under an "open range" system. This meant livestock could roam freely, and farm owners were responsible for fencing them in. However, this open range system has been replaced by a "closed range" system, which shifts fencing responsibility to livestock owners.

In closed-range states, if your livestock wander and damage a neighbor's crops, you bear full liability for the loss. However, in some states, the injured party must prove negligence to recover damages.

Highway Accidents

Animals may break loose and trespass onto public roads, such as highways. In most cases, especially those involving personal injury, the injured party must prove the livestock owner was negligent.

However, the defense will also attempt to prove the driver was negligent. The driver must demonstrate they were exercising extraordinary caution while driving. The court will also assess other factors, such as whether the animal was within sight, whether the driver was speeding, or whether the driver was distracted.

Defenses You Can Raise

Based on existing facts and your attorney's advice, you may raise the following defenses against liability for escaped animals:

  • Proper containment: The animals were properly restrained, and the fence was properly installed and maintained. This defense may require testimony from an "expert" who has handled the specific animals.
  • Plaintiff negligence: The injured party's negligent behavior caused the accident or incident.
  • Third-party interference: A third party (such as a reckless driver or vandal) tampered with or damaged the fence, causing the animals to escape.
