10 Common Mistakes to Avoid When Installing Wire Mesh Fencing

November 20, 2025 7 min read
Back to Blog & News

Mistake 1-3: Planning and Preparation Errors

The foundation of any successful fencing project lies in thorough planning and preparation, yet this is where many installers — both professional and DIY — make their first critical errors. Failing to check local zoning regulations and property lines before installation can lead to costly disputes with neighbors, forced removal of non-compliant fencing, and potential legal liabilities. Many municipalities have specific requirements regarding fence height, setback distances from property boundaries, and permitted materials, and these regulations vary significantly between residential, commercial, and industrial zones. Not accounting for ground slope and terrain variations in post spacing is another common oversight that results in uneven fence lines, visible gaps at the bottom of the fence, and structural weakness at transition points between level and sloped sections. On uneven terrain, posts must be staggered in height or stepped to follow the contour of the land while maintaining a consistent top line. Ordering insufficient materials without including a 10% waste allowance for cuts and mistakes is a planning error that can bring an entire installation project to a halt. Running out of mesh fabric, tension wire, or fittings mid-project leads to delays, mismatched material batches, and additional shipping costs that could have been easily avoided with accurate upfront material calculations.

Mistake 4-6: Post Installation Errors

Post installation is the structural backbone of any wire mesh fence, and errors made during this phase compromise the entire system. Setting posts at insufficient depth is perhaps the most common and consequential mistake. For residential applications, posts should be embedded a minimum of 600mm in concrete footings, while commercial and high-security installations require a minimum depth of 900mm to withstand wind loads, impact forces, and tension from the mesh fabric. Posts set too shallow will lean, shift, or fail completely under stress, particularly in areas with high winds or expansive soils. Not using a string line to ensure straight post alignment results in a visually unprofessional fence with irregular spacing and uneven tension distribution along the fence line. A string line stretched between terminal posts provides a straight reference that ensures all intermediate posts are set in a perfectly aligned position. Failing to brace terminal posts adequately against tension loads is another critical error. Terminal posts — those at the ends, corners, and gates of a fence line — bear the entire tension load of the stretched mesh fabric and must be braced with diagonal struts, concrete footings of adequate diameter, or both. Without proper bracing, terminal posts will lean inward under tension, causing the entire fence line to sag and eventually fail.

Mistake 7-8: Fabric and Tension Errors

Installing mesh fabric with the diamond pattern oriented incorrectly is a surprisingly frequent mistake that affects both the appearance and performance of chain link fencing. The diamond pattern should run with the twist barbs pointing upward — this orientation allows the fabric to shed water and debris naturally, prevents the accumulation of leaves and snow that can add weight and accelerate corrosion, and provides a cleaner visual appearance. When installed upside down, the barbs create small cups that trap moisture and organic material against the wire surface, significantly accelerating corrosion at every intersection point. Not applying proper tension to the fabric is equally damaging. Chain link mesh should be stretched taut with no visible sag between posts, typically achieved using a fence stretcher or come-along tool that pulls the fabric to its specified tension before securing it to the terminal post with tension bars and bands.

Proper fabric tension is the single most important factor in chain link fence performance. A correctly tensioned fence should produce a sharp, metallic ring when struck — if the fabric flexes visibly or produces a dull thud, it is under-tensioned and will sag progressively over time. Always tension fabric in the direction of the weave, never across it, and never cut tension wire until the fabric is fully secured.

Mistake 9-10: Finishing and Hardware Errors

The finishing phase of a fence installation is often rushed, leading to premature failures that could have been prevented with minimal additional effort and cost. Using incorrect or low-quality fittings that corrode quickly is a mistake that compromises the entire fence structure. Galvanized tension bands, brace bands, carriage bolts, and nuts should all be made from hot-dip galvanized steel matching or exceeding the corrosion resistance of the fence fabric itself. Mixing dissimilar metals — such as using plain steel bolts with galvanized fittings — creates galvanic corrosion cells that rapidly deteriorate the less noble metal, leading to loose connections and structural failure. Similarly, using low-cost import fittings with thin galvanizing or no galvanizing at all will result in rust staining and hardware failure within just a few years, even in moderate climates. Neglecting to apply rust preventative coating to all cut wire ends and post tops after installation is the final common mistake. Every cut made to mesh fabric, tension wire, or posts during installation exposes bare steel that will rust immediately upon exposure to moisture. Applying a cold galvanizing compound or zinc-rich paint to all cut ends, and fitting post caps to protect the open tops of line and terminal posts, seals these vulnerable points and extends the service life of the entire fence system by years.

Professional Installation Recommendations

While many wire mesh fencing projects are well within the capabilities of experienced DIY installers, certain situations strongly warrant professional installation. Projects involving slopes exceeding 15 degrees, fence heights above 2.4 meters, commercial or industrial security requirements, or specialized configurations such as cantilever gates and anti-climb toppings should be entrusted to qualified fencing contractors with the equipment, experience, and knowledge to ensure correct installation. Regardless of whether you choose to install yourself or hire a professional, always follow the manufacturer's guidelines for the specific product being installed. These guidelines specify post spacing, footing dimensions, tension requirements, and hardware compatibility that are essential for achieving the rated performance and service life of the product.
